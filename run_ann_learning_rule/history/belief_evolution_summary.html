Belief Evolution Analysis

Research project: Biologically Plausible Learning Rules for Recurrent Neural Networks
Analysis covers snapshots bk1 through bk6 plus current research_state.md (7 states total, 6 experiments)

What this document tracks: This analysis traces how the research agent's scientific beliefs evolved across iterative experiments. It identifies cases where the agent drew conclusions that were later overturned by new evidence (including bugs and artifacts), and summarizes the validated scientific findings that survived scrutiny. The goal is to understand the epistemic trajectory of the research -- not just what was learned, but how understanding was built, revised, and sometimes corrected.

Scientific Conclusions (Validated Findings)

The Central Discovery: Trace Quality is Everything

The single most important finding across all experiments is that the quality of the eligibility trace (the forward-mode gradient approximation) is the dominant factor determining whether a biologically plausible learning rule can match BPTT performance. Specifically:

  • Full RTRL (exact forward-mode gradient) + random feedback + plain SGD achieves 100% accuracy on the copy task, perfectly matching BPTT+Adam.
  • Rank-1 trace approximation (RFLO) caps at ~28-34% regardless of optimizer, feedback mechanism, or hyperparameters.
  • Random feedback alignment works perfectly when the gradient signal is correct -- it is NOT a bottleneck.
  • The optimizer (SGD vs Adam vs Momentum) is irrelevant when gradient information is correct.

How Understanding Built Incrementally

Phase 1 (Exp01-02): Established that RFLO has a fundamental performance ceiling (~34%) on memory tasks. This is not a hyperparameter issue -- it persists across all settings tested. BPTT reaches 100% with proper configuration (alpha=1.0).
Phase 2 (Exp03-04): Through ablation, identified that the rank-1 trace approximation (not random feedback, not the optimizer) is the sole bottleneck. This required correcting two false beliefs: (1) that random feedback was a problem, and (2) that the optimizer mattered.
Phase 3 (Exp05): Explored tractable RTRL approximations. Found that spatial partitioning (block-diagonal) requires unrealistically large blocks (K=32 for n=64), and random projection completely destroys gradient signal. Cross-neuron influence propagation is the critical information that simple approximations lose.

The Final Scientific Picture

The research has established a clear hierarchy of importance for biologically plausible RNN learning:

  1. Trace quality (gradient approximation accuracy) dominates everything. Full RTRL proves that if you have the exact forward-mode gradient, all other concerns (feedback mechanism, optimizer) vanish. The question is: how much can you approximate RTRL before it breaks?
  2. Random feedback alignment is a solved problem for RNNs. Contrary to initial expectations, random backward weights work perfectly when paired with good gradient information. This validates feedback alignment as biologically plausible without loss.
  3. Simple spatial approximations to RTRL are insufficient at biologically realistic scales. Block-diagonal RTRL only works with blocks covering half the network. Random projections destroy the signal entirely. The critical information is how influence propagates across neurons over time.
  4. The open frontier is temporal approximation. Truncated forward-RTRL (propagating the full Jacobian for K steps then resetting) is the most promising unexplored direction. It is online, causal, and could map to biological oscillation cycles.

Self-Corrections

These are cases where the agent believed something, then reversed its position based on new evidence.

1. "Random Feedback is a Significant Bottleneck" (H4: 55% to FALSE)

Initial belief (bk1-bk4): The agent assumed that random feedback alignment was a meaningful limitation, since the original RFLO paper and feedback alignment literature focused on this as a key challenge. Confidence held at 50-55% for four snapshots.

What happened: Exp03 showed that exact feedback (C2=26.5%) performed equally or worse than random feedback (C1=28.4%). Exp04 definitively confirmed: Full RTRL + random feedback + SGD = 100%.

Correction: Random feedback is completely irrelevant when gradient information (trace) is correct. The agent had imported an assumption from the feedforward literature that did not apply in this recurrent setting.

Lesson: Assumptions from adjacent literature (feedforward nets) do not automatically transfer to the recurrent setting. Empirical testing was essential.

2. "The Optimizer (Adam) is the Real Bottleneck" (H8: 65% to FALSE)

Initial belief (bk5): After Exp03 showed that even full RTRL+SGD only reached 40% (while BPTT+Adam reached 100%), the agent hypothesized that Adam's second-order statistics were the dominant factor. Confidence: 65%.

What happened: This conclusion was drawn from a BUGGED experiment. Exp04 showed that full RTRL+SGD actually reaches 100%. The Exp03 result (40% for RTRL+SGD) was an implementation error.

Correction: The optimizer is completely irrelevant. SGD, momentum, and Adam all reach 100% with full RTRL. The agent was misled by buggy experimental results into proposing an elaborate "biological momentum" theory that was entirely unnecessary.

Lesson: Anomalous results should be replicated before building theories on them. The agent correctly flagged C4's anomalous result as suspicious but still built H8 on the C3 result without questioning it.

3. "BPTT Itself May Be Limited by Architecture" (H6: 40% to RESOLVED)

Initial belief (bk3): When Exp01 showed BPTT at only 73.5% accuracy on a simple copy task, the agent briefly entertained that the vanilla RNN architecture might be fundamentally limiting even for BPTT.

What happened: Exp02 showed that the issue was simply alpha=0.2 (the leak rate), which caused information to decay before it needed to be recalled. With alpha=1.0 (no leak), BPTT reaches 100%.

Correction: This was a hyperparameter misconfiguration, not a fundamental limit. The agent correctly identified and resolved this within one iteration.

4. "Block-Diagonal RTRL Maps to Cortical Columns" (H11: 60% to 30%)

Initial belief (bk6): The agent proposed that block-diagonal RTRL (neurons in groups tracking only within-group Jacobians) naturally maps to biological cortical columns/minicolumns.

What happened: Exp05 showed that biologically realistic column sizes (K=4-8 neurons) give no improvement over simple RFLO (26-30%). Only K=32 (half the network of 64) shows meaningful improvement (79%).

Correction: The biological metaphor does not hold because real cortical columns are too small to capture the cross-neuron influence paths that make RTRL work. The agent's analogy was seductive but empirically unsupported.

5. Confidence in H1 Steadily Eroded (55% to FALSE)

Trajectory: 55% (bk1) -> 60% (bk2, strengthened by literature) -> 45% (bk3, weakened by Exp01) -> 30% (bk4, weakened by Exp02) -> 20% (bk5) -> RESOLVED FALSE (bk6).

Pattern: The initial hypothesis that simple eligibility traces could compete with BPTT was gradually eroded by accumulating experimental evidence. The agent initially expected the gap to be small (literature suggested RFLO "works"), but empirical testing revealed a fundamental performance ceiling. Notably, the confidence INCREASED briefly after the literature review (bk2: 60%) before the first experimental reality check brought it down.

Lesson: Literature-based confidence can be misleading -- papers often test on tasks that favor their method. Empirical replication on a standardized task was essential to reveal the true limitations.

Bug-Induced Artifacts

These are cases where experimental results were initially trusted but later found to be caused by implementation errors.

Bug 1: Exp03 RTRL Implementation Error

The bug: In Exp03 (ablation experiment), the full RTRL condition (C3: random FB + full RTRL) achieved only 40.4% accuracy. In Exp04, the identical configuration achieved 100%. The bk6 state explicitly notes: "Exp03's C3 result (40%) was likely due to an implementation issue."

Suspected cause: The bk6 state mentions "possibly in the einsum or the loop where we accumulate dW_rec across batch incorrectly." The exact bug was not fully diagnosed in the logs but was corrected in the Exp04 reimplementation.

False conclusions drawn:

  • H8 (optimizer is the bottleneck): Because RTRL+SGD appeared to only reach 40% while BPTT+Adam reached 100%, the agent concluded that Adam's adaptive learning rates were the key advantage. This led to speculation about "biologically plausible momentum" and "synaptic metaplasticity" -- an entire research direction that was later shown to be based on a bug.
  • H7 partially suppressed: The true answer (trace quality is EVERYTHING) was partially obscured because the bugged experiment made it look like even a perfect trace was insufficient without a good optimizer.

How detected: The anomalous C4 result (13.8% -- exact FB + full RTRL, which should be best but was worst) raised suspicion. The agent flagged this as "likely a learning rate issue or sign error." This prompted Exp04 as a complete redo with careful implementation, which revealed the full picture.

Impact: One full research iteration (Step 4 to Step 5) was spent pursuing wrong conclusions. However, the agent's self-correction instinct (flagging C4 as anomalous) ultimately led to the fix.

Bug 2: Alpha=0.2 Misconfiguration in Exp01

The bug: Exp01 used alpha=0.2 (leaky RNN with fast decay) for a copy task that requires holding information for 10+ timesteps. This caused BPTT itself to only reach 73.5% accuracy, making it appear that the task was intrinsically difficult for the architecture.

False conclusions drawn:

  • H6: Brief belief that the RNN architecture itself might be limiting.
  • Underestimation of the RFLO gap: With BPTT at 73.5% and RFLO at 29.7%, the gap appeared to be ~44%. The true gap (BPTT=100%, RFLO=34%) is actually ~66% -- significantly larger.

How detected: The agent noticed that 73.5% on a seq_len=10 copy task "should be solvable near-perfectly" and hypothesized the alpha parameter. Exp02 confirmed this by sweeping alpha values.

Impact: Minor -- the agent correctly identified this within one iteration and it did not lead to a major wrong turn.

Lessons Learned About Research Epistemics

1. Buggy Experiments Generate Plausible-Sounding Wrong Theories

The Exp03 bug is instructive: the 40% RTRL result was not obviously wrong. It was plausible that even exact gradients would struggle without an adaptive optimizer. The agent built a coherent theory (H8: "biological momentum") on this faulty foundation. The lesson is that anomalous results should be replicated before theorizing -- especially when one condition in an experiment is clearly wrong (C4=13.8%), the OTHER conditions may also be unreliable.

2. Literature Confidence Can Be Misleading

After the literature review (bk2), H1's confidence INCREASED (55% to 60%) because RFLO was reported to "work" in the original paper. But the original paper tested on different tasks (continuous outputs, not discrete memory). When tested on a standardized copy task, RFLO was revealed to be fundamentally limited. Papers select tasks that make their methods look good.

3. Ablation Design Matters Enormously

The research could have been much faster if Exp03 had been implemented correctly. The ablation design was correct (2x2: feedback type x trace type), but the implementation was buggy, leading to one wasted iteration. Critical ablation experiments should be implemented with extra care, or replicated immediately.

4. The Simplest Explanation Often Wins

The final answer is remarkably clean: trace quality is everything, nothing else matters. But the path to this conclusion involved entertaining complex multi-factor theories (optimizer + feedback + trace interactions). Occam's razor should be applied more aggressively -- when one factor dominates, the others are usually irrelevant.

5. Biological Analogies Should Be Tested Empirically, Not Just Argued

The "cortical columns = block-diagonal RTRL" analogy (H11) was intellectually appealing but empirically false at realistic scales. The agent correctly tested it rather than just asserting it. Biological metaphors need quantitative validation.

6. Self-Correction Works When Anomalies Are Flagged

The agent's strongest epistemic move was flagging the C4 anomaly in Exp03. Even though it initially drew wrong conclusions from C3, the suspicion about C4 led directly to the corrective Exp04. Anomaly detection is a critical research skill.

7. Negative Results Are Highly Informative

Exp05's random projection failure (12-17% -- worse than chance) is as informative as any positive result. It definitively rules out an entire class of approximation strategies and points toward the specific structure (cross-neuron temporal influence paths) that must be preserved. Clear failures narrow the search space efficiently.

Summary of Research Trajectory

The research progressed through three conceptual phases:

  1. Establishing the problem (Exp01-02): RFLO (the simplest bio-plausible rule) has a fundamental ~34% ceiling on memory tasks. BPTT reaches 100% with proper configuration. The gap is real and large.
  2. Finding the bottleneck (Exp03-04): Through ablation (including one buggy iteration that required correction), definitively established that trace quality alone determines performance. Random feedback and optimizer choice are irrelevant. This is the key scientific insight.
  3. Seeking tractable approximations (Exp05+): Simple spatial approximations (block-diagonal, random projection) are insufficient. The field needs temporal approximations that preserve cross-neuron influence propagation. Truncated forward-RTRL is the leading candidate.

The research has successfully identified both the problem (rank-1 traces are insufficient) and the constraint on solutions (cross-neuron influence paths must be preserved), positioning the next phase to design a novel algorithm that satisfies both biological plausibility and computational tractability.
