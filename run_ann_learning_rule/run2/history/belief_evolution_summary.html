Belief Evolution Analysis

Tracking the evolution of scientific beliefs across 8 research iterations for the project: "Biologically Plausible Learning Algorithm for RNNs"

What this document tracks: This analysis traces how a research agent's beliefs evolved over 8 iterative research steps (snapshots bk1 through bk8, plus the final state). It identifies hypotheses that were initially believed but later disproven, experiments whose results were misinterpreted or caused pivots, and the final validated scientific conclusions that survived scrutiny.

Research goal: Design a novel, biologically plausible learning rule for recurrent neural networks that handles long temporal dependencies where Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT) fails.

1. Scientific Conclusions (Validated)

Final Validated Finding: Multi-Timescale Eligibility (MTE)

The research conclusively demonstrated that organizing neurons into frequency bands with matched eligibility decay rates creates a biologically plausible learning rule that outperforms BPTT in the long-temporal-dependency regime.

Key quantitative results (trustworthy, from experiment_v5 and v6):

  • Copy task, delay=50: MTE accuracy = 0.238 vs BPTT = 0.125 (random/failure). MTE is the only method that learns.
  • Copy task, delay=30: MTE accuracy = 0.255 vs BPTT = 0.125 (fails) vs e-prop = 0.236. MTE wins by +8%.
  • Adding problem, length=100: MTE MSE = 0.077 vs BPTT = 0.169 (random/failure). BPTT completely fails; MTE solves the task.
  • Short delays (d=10-20): BPTT still wins, as expected for short-range credit assignment.

The mechanism: Slow-band neurons (lambda=0.998) retain 78% of eligibility at t-50, while standard single-timescale (lambda=0.95) retains only 7.7%. This explains the dramatic advantage at long delays.

Biological narrative: Different neuron types in cortex have different temporal integration constants matched to their functional role. MTE makes this an explicit design principle for learning.

Secondary Confirmed Finding: Phase Mechanisms Do Not Help Credit Assignment

Three distinct attempts to use oscillatory phase for temporal credit assignment all failed:

  1. OPCR (phase-as-address): Phase bin indexing fragments the eligibility signal, reducing SNR.
  2. PRE (phase-resonant decay): Modulating decay by phase alignment shows no benefit.
  3. PFSC phase synchronization: Within-band phase sync adds no value to multi-timescale learning.

This is a negative result but a scientifically valid one: oscillatory phase, while important for neural coding, does not appear to be useful as a credit assignment mechanism in RNN learning rules.

Tertiary Finding: BPTT Fails Catastrophically at Long Delays

BPTT drops to random-chance performance at delay >= 30 on the copy task and length >= 100 on the adding problem. This is due to vanishing gradients. Local rules (even simple e-prop) remain above chance, and multi-timescale rules significantly outperform. This validates the research premise.

4. Self-Corrections

Major Self-Correction #1: Phase Does Not Help Credit Assignment

Original belief (bk1-bk4): Oscillatory phase relationships between neurons could serve as a "temporal addressing system" for credit assignment, providing structured temporal reference that eligibility traces lack. Confidence peaked at 55%.

What happened: The ablation study in Step 4 (bk5) revealed that removing the phase mechanism entirely (no-phase ablation) performed slightly better than the full OPCR algorithm (0.271 vs 0.261 accuracy). The phase bins were fragmenting the eligibility signal rather than concentrating it.

Self-correction moment: The agent explicitly acknowledged: "Phase selectivity does NOT help -- the no-phase ablation slightly outperforms OPCR." Confidence dropped from 55% to 15% in a single step.

How this led to progress: The failure diagnosis revealed the real bottleneck was not the phase mechanism but the inherent weakness of feedback alignment + simple eligibility traces. This led to the pivotal insight about targeting the long-delay regime.

Major Self-Correction #2: Targeting Wrong Regime

Original belief (bk1-bk5): The algorithm should be competitive with BPTT on standard tasks (sequence length 10-50, short delays). The goal was "within reasonable range of BPTT."

What happened: Step 5 experiments showed that at delay=20 where BPTT completely fails (vanishing gradients, drops to random), local rules still learn. This revealed that local biologically plausible rules have a fundamental advantage at long delays, not just a competitive disadvantage at short ones.

Self-correction: The agent pivoted the entire research strategy: "Rather than trying to make phase help a fundamentally weak base rule, we should focus on the regime where BPTT fails." This strategic reframing was the turning point of the project.

Self-Correction #3: Factored Eligibility Too Weak

Belief (bk6): PFSC with factored eligibility (trace_i * pre_j) would work well across all delays. Confidence 45%.

What happened: PFSC showed breakthrough performance at d=50 (the only method learning) but underperformed e-prop at d=10-30. The factored approximation loses pairwise structure needed for short delays.

Correction: Combined full pairwise eligibility from e-prop with the multi-timescale structure from PFSC to create MTE -- getting the best of both approaches.

Self-Correction #4: Phase Synchronization Within Bands

Belief: Phase synchronization between neurons in the same frequency band would help coordinate their learning.

Evidence: PFSC vs PFSC-noSync comparison showed no benefit (0.156 vs 0.153 at d=50). The agent correctly concluded: "Phase synchronization component adds no value."

Result: Dropped the synchronization mechanism and focused purely on multi-timescale structure.

5. Bug-Induced Artifacts

Artifact #1: Overly Optimistic Initial OPCR Result (Step 3)

The artifact: In Step 3 (bk4), OPCR showed accuracy of 0.181 on the copy task vs random baseline of 0.125. This was interpreted as "algorithm is functional" and "the gap between OPCR and BPTT on copy is modest, which is encouraging."

The problem: This was not a "bug" per se, but an artifact of insufficient training (only 3000 steps) combined with weak baselines. The BPTT baseline itself was only 0.208 -- far below what BPTT should achieve on copy with delay=10 (later shown to be 0.508 with proper training). Both methods were essentially in an early-training regime where random noise gives false signal.

False conclusion drawn: "OPCR is functional and competitive with BPTT" -- in reality, both were barely above random with insufficient training, and OPCR was fundamentally flawed.

How detected: Extended training to 10K steps in Step 4 revealed BPTT reaches 0.873 while OPCR plateaus at 0.261 -- a 3.3x gap, not the "modest" gap initially suggested.

Artifact #2: Loss Scale Discrepancy

The artifact: In Step 3, OPCR loss was 0.897 while BPTT loss was 0.106 -- an 8x difference.

Root cause: The phase credit computation was not properly normalized, leading to excessively large weight updates. This was identified as a "signal scaling issue in the phase credit computation."

Impact: Made OPCR appear more functional than it was (accuracy above random despite bad optimization dynamics), and obscured the fundamental problem that phase gating was harmful.

Artifact #3: Softmax Phase Kernel Issue

The artifact: The softmax-normalized phase kernels used to distribute eligibility across M bins were causing most bins to receive near-zero eligibility.

Diagnosis (from bk5): "Softmax kills gradients: Softmax normalization means most bins get near-zero eligibility, potentially zeroing out the correct one."

Impact: This implementation choice turned the phase mechanism from potentially helpful to actively harmful -- it dispersed rather than concentrated the learning signal.

6. How Understanding Built Up Incrementally

Iteration 1-2 (bk1-bk2): Identified gap in literature (phase credit routing never tried), formulated hypothesis, verified novelty. Established the research direction.
Iteration 3 (bk3): Formalized OPCR into complete mathematical framework. First implementation showed weak but nonzero learning signal, providing false encouragement.
Iteration 4 (bk4-bk5): CRITICAL FAILURE. Extended testing + ablation revealed phase mechanism is useless or harmful. This was the most informative experiment -- the ablation that compared "with phase" vs "without phase" provided definitive negative evidence. THREE key insights emerged: (1) phase doesn't help, (2) the base rule (eligibility + FA) is inherently weak, (3) need to reconsider what "competitive" means.
Iteration 5 (bk5-bk6): STRATEGIC PIVOT. Discovery that local rules outperform BPTT at very long delays (d=20+) reframed the entire research question. Instead of "how to match BPTT," the question became "how to extend temporal reach of local rules into the regime where BPTT fails."
Iteration 6 (bk6-bk7): BREAKTHROUGH. Multi-timescale insight: neurons with different eligibility decay rates naturally form a "spectral decomposition" of temporal credit. PFSC demonstrated this works at d=50. Key weakness identified (factored eligibility too weak at short delays).
Iteration 7-8 (bk7-final): VALIDATION. Combined pairwise eligibility (e-prop strength) with multi-timescale structure (PFSC strength) to create MTE. Validated on adding problem at length=100 -- the strongest result, showing BPTT fails while MTE succeeds.

7. Lessons Learned (Research Epistemics)

Lesson 1: Ablation Studies Are Essential -- Not Optional

The research spent iterations 1-3 developing and initially testing OPCR without an ablation. It took until iteration 4 to compare "with phase" vs "without phase." Had this ablation been run from the start (even at 3K steps), it would have revealed the phase mechanism is useless immediately, saving 1-2 iterations.

Principle: Always run the minimal ablation alongside the first test. The question "does my mechanism actually help compared to the same system without it?" is the most important early-stage experiment.

Lesson 2: Insufficient Training Creates False Signals

The 3000-step initial experiment gave a misleading picture: BPTT at 0.208 and OPCR at 0.181 looked "modestly competitive." With proper 10K-step training, the true gap was 0.873 vs 0.261 -- a completely different story. Short training runs can make random noise look like signal.

Principle: Ensure baselines are run to convergence before drawing conclusions about relative performance. A weak baseline makes everything look competitive.

Lesson 3: Negative Results Contain Positive Information

The failure of phase mechanisms was not a dead end -- it contained crucial information. The observation that "at delay=20, BPTT fails but local rules don't" was discovered because of the extended comparison experiments run to diagnose phase failure. The failure led directly to the strategic pivot that made the project succeed.

Principle: When an experiment fails, analyze WHY it failed in detail. The diagnosis often contains the seed of the next breakthrough.

Lesson 4: Reframe the Problem When the Solution Is Not Working

The original framing was "match BPTT at short delays." After multiple failures, the agent reframed to "beat BPTT at long delays where it fails." This reframing transformed a series of failures into a success story. The algorithm's weakness (can't match BPTT at short delays) became irrelevant; its strength (works where BPTT fails) became the headline.

Principle: If your approach cannot compete in the established benchmark regime, ask whether there is a different regime where it uniquely excels. Playing to your strengths often beats fixing your weaknesses.

Lesson 5: Build Incrementally on What Works

The final algorithm (MTE) was not designed from scratch -- it was assembled from proven components: pairwise eligibility (from e-prop, known to work) + multi-timescale decay (from PFSC, proven at d=50) + random feedback (standard biologically plausible component). Each piece had been individually validated before combination.

Principle: When constructing a novel algorithm, combine components that have individually been shown to work, rather than designing everything de novo.

Lesson 6: The "Novelty" That Works May Be Simpler Than Expected

The agent initially sought a complex, "surprising" mechanism (oscillatory phase credit routing with cross-frequency coupling). The mechanism that actually worked (per-neuron decay rates organized into bands) is conceptually much simpler. The agent noted this tension: "may be seen as just varying lambda per neuron." But the empirical result (beating BPTT on a standard benchmark) speaks louder than mechanism complexity.

Principle: Prefer the simplest mechanism that produces a significant result over a complex mechanism that sounds impressive but doesn't work.
