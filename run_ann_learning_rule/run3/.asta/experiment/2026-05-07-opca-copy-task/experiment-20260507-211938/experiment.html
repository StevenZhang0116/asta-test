Abstract

We implement and evaluate the Oscillatory Phase Credit Assignment (OPCA) learning rule, a biologically plausible algorithm for training recurrent neural networks (RNNs) that proposes to multiplex forward computation and credit assignment through the same synaptic weights using oscillatory phase dynamics. The core idea is that during a "credit phase," error signals propagate backward through time using the forward weight matrix W directly (without transposition), in contrast to standard backpropagation through time (BPTT) which requires W-transpose. We test OPCA and four related methods -- BPTT (gold standard), Feedback Alignment (FA), RFLO-like random feedback, and a variant OPCA-alpha with learned damping -- on the copy task, where a vanilla RNN with 128 hidden units must memorize 8 binary values across a 10-step delay and reproduce them. Training runs for 5000 iterations with batch size 32, using Adam optimization and a learning rate search over {0.001, 0.005, 0.01}. BPTT achieves near-perfect performance (100% bit accuracy, MSE=0.0004, converging at iteration 650). Standard OPCA reaches only 62.4% accuracy (MSE=0.234), performing worse than Feedback Alignment (79.7%, MSE=0.145) -- a counterintuitive result suggesting that using the structured forward weight matrix W for credit propagation may be less effective than a fixed random matrix for this task. The OPCA-alpha variant, which learns the damping factor (converging from 0.9 to 0.047), achieves the best performance among biologically plausible methods (80.6%, MSE=0.142). No biologically plausible method reached the 0.01 MSE threshold within 5000 iterations. These results suggest that while OPCA provides an interesting biological mechanism for credit assignment, its performance on a basic temporal memory task falls substantially short of BPTT, and the hypothesis that using forward weights W improves over random feedback is not supported by this experiment. Further investigation on diverse tasks and longer training is needed before stronger conclusions can be drawn.

Introduction

Training recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to solve temporal sequence tasks requires propagating credit backward through time -- attributing present errors to past weight activations. The standard algorithm, backpropagation through time (BPTT), accomplishes this by unrolling the network and computing exact gradients, requiring the transposed weight matrix W-transpose during the backward pass. While highly effective, BPTT poses a fundamental challenge for biological plausibility: real neurons do not appear to have access to a separate "backward pathway" that mirrors the forward connectivity with transposed weights. This is known as the weight transport problem. Several biologically motivated alternatives have been proposed to circumvent the weight transport problem. Feedback Alignment (Lillicrap et al., 2016) replaces W-transpose with a fixed random matrix B, showing that approximate credit signals can still drive learning. RFLO and related methods explore online, local learning rules. More recently, oscillatory dynamics in the brain -- particularly the coupling between theta and gamma rhythms -- have been proposed as a potential mechanism for separating forward computation from credit assignment in time, using oscillatory phase to gate when each process occurs. The Oscillatory Phase Credit Assignment (OPCA) algorithm formalizes this intuition. During a "forward phase," the RNN computes its normal hidden state dynamics and stores eligibility traces. During a "credit phase," error signals propagate backward through time using the forward weight matrix W itself (without transposition), with a damping factor alpha controlling how far credit propagates. The weight update is a Hebbian-like product of the credit signal and the eligibility trace. The appeal of OPCA is that it proposes a concrete biological mechanism -- oscillatory phase-gating -- that could allow the same physical synapses to serve dual roles in computation and learning. In this work, we implement OPCA from scratch in PyTorch and evaluate it on the copy task, a standard RNN benchmark requiring temporal memory over a delay period. We compare OPCA against BPTT (gold standard), Feedback Alignment, RFLO-like random feedback, and a variant OPCA-alpha in which the damping factor alpha is learned rather than fixed. All methods use the Adam optimizer to ensure fair comparison of the learning rule itself rather than optimization dynamics. We conduct a learning rate search over {0.001, 0.005, 0.01} and train for 5000 iterations with batch size 32. Our main findings are as follows. BPTT achieves near-perfect performance, confirming the task is solvable. Standard OPCA reaches only 62.4% bit accuracy, falling below Feedback Alignment (79.7%) -- a counterintuitive result, since OPCA uses the structured forward weights rather than a random matrix. The OPCA-alpha variant, which learns to reduce its damping factor from 0.9 to approximately 0.047, achieves the best performance among biologically plausible methods (80.6%). RFLO performs worst (51.6%). None of the biologically plausible methods converge to the 0.01 MSE threshold within 5000 iterations, while BPTT reaches it at iteration 650. These results provide a preliminary characterization of OPCA's behavior on one task. We caution that a single benchmark is insufficient to draw broad conclusions, and that the observed performance gap between OPCA and BPTT may reflect task-specific properties, implementation choices, or hyperparameter sensitivity rather than fundamental limitations of the algorithm. We report our findings conservatively and highlight directions for further investigation.

Approach

TASK: COPY TASK We evaluate all methods on the copy task, a standard benchmark for RNN temporal memory. The task structure is as follows: - Input phase (timesteps 0-7): An 8-dimensional binary sequence is presented at each timestep, along with a "go signal" channel set to 0. The same sequence is repeated across all 8 input timesteps, giving the network ample opportunity to encode it. - Delay phase (timesteps 8-17): All inputs are zero; the network must maintain the sequence in its hidden state. - Output phase (timesteps 18-25): The go signal is set to 1; the network must reproduce the original 8 binary values. Loss is computed only during this phase. Total sequence length: 26 timesteps. Input dimensionality: 9 (8 binary channels + 1 go signal). Output dimensionality: 8. Targets are binary {0, 1} values, and loss is mean squared error (MSE). Bit accuracy is computed by thresholding network outputs at 0.5. Each training batch consists of independently sampled random binary sequences. For a batch of size 32, input tensors have shape (26, 32, 9) and target tensors have shape (26, 32, 8). ARCHITECTURE: VANILLA RNN All methods use a vanilla RNN with the following structure: Hidden state update: a_t = W_rec * h_{t-1} + W_in * x_t + b_rec h_t = tanh(a_t) Output: y_t = W_out * h_t + b_out Parameters: - W_rec: 128 x 128 (recurrent weights) - W_in: 128 x 9 (input weights) - b_rec: 128 (recurrent bias) - W_out: 8 x 128 (output weights) - b_out: 8 (output bias) - Total: 18,696 parameters All weights initialized with scaled random normal (scale 0.1); biases initialized to zero. Fixed random seed (42) ensures identical initialization across methods. LEARNING METHODS 1. BPTT (Backpropagation Through Time) The gold standard baseline. Standard PyTorch autograd computes exact gradients by unrolling the RNN. Gradients are clipped to norm 1.0. Weights updated with Adam optimizer. 2. OPCA (Oscillatory Phase Credit Assignment) Implements the proposed biologically plausible learning rule in three phases: Forward phase: Run the RNN forward, storing at each timestep t: - Hidden state h_t - Previous hidden state h_{t-1} (eligibility trace presynaptic component) - Tanh derivative: dtanh_t = 1 - h_t^2 (eligibility trace postsynaptic component) - Input x_t Output error computation: For timesteps in the output phase, delta_y_t = (2 / N) * (y_t - target_t) [MSE gradient, N = total output elements] delta_h_t = delta_y_t @ W_out [project to hidden space] Credit phase: Propagate credit backward through time using W_rec (no transpose): c_T = 0 (initialize) For t = T-1 down to 0: c_t = alpha * (c_{t+1} @ W_rec) + delta_h_t c_t_modulated = c_t * dtanh_t [apply tanh derivative] Note: The credit propagation uses W_rec WITHOUT transposition (c @ W_rec in batch form), in contrast to BPTT which effectively uses W_rec transposed. This is the defining property of OPCA. Weight update: dW_rec = sum_t (c_t_modulated^T @ h_{t-1}) / batch_size dW_in = sum_t (c_t_modulated^T @ x_t) / batch_size db_rec = sum_t mean(c_t_modulated) dW_out = sum_t (delta_y_t^T @ h_t) / batch_size [standard output gradient] db_out = sum_t mean(delta_y_t) All weight updates applied using Adam optimizer with the manually computed gradients. Fixed alpha = 0.9. 3. Feedback Alignment (FA) Identical to OPCA but replaces W_rec in the credit propagation with a fixed random matrix B, initialized once at training start (scaled random normal, scale 0.1) and never updated: c_t = alpha * (c_{t+1} @ B) + delta_h_t All other computations (forward pass, weight updates, Adam optimizer) identical to OPCA. 4. RFLO-like Random Feedback Similar to FA but the feedback matrix is re-sampled randomly at every training iteration: B_iter ~ N(0, 0.1^2 I) [new random matrix each iteration] c_t = alpha * (c_{t+1} @ B_iter) + delta_h_t This serves as a control to isolate the benefit of a fixed vs. changing feedback matrix. 5. OPCA-alpha (Learned Damping Factor) Identical to OPCA but treats alpha as a learnable parameter. Alpha is reparameterized as alpha = sigmoid(log_alpha) to keep it in (0, 1). The gradient of the loss with respect to log_alpha is approximated via a chain-rule expansion through the credit propagation, and log_alpha is updated using Adam with learning rate 0.01. Initialized at alpha = 0.9. OPTIMIZER A key design choice is that all custom learning rules (OPCA, FA, RFLO, OPCA-alpha) use Adam momentum for their weight updates, not plain SGD. This was found to be critical: preliminary experiments with plain SGD showed all biologically plausible methods barely learning, while Adam-based updates produced meaningful convergence. The Adam hyperparameters are: beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, epsilon=1e-8. For BPTT, the standard PyTorch Adam optimizer is used with gradient clipping (max norm=1.0). EXPERIMENTAL PROTOCOL - Training iterations: 5000 per run - Batch size: 32 - Learning rate search: {0.001, 0.005, 0.01} - Best learning rate selected per method based on final MSE loss - Loss and accuracy recorded every 50 iterations (100 checkpoints total) - Random seed: 42 for all methods (same initial weights) - Hardware: NVIDIA GPU (CUDA 13.0), PyTorch 2.11.0 METRICS - Final MSE loss: Mean squared error on the output phase at the last recorded checkpoint - Minimum MSE: Lowest MSE achieved at any checkpoint during training - Convergence speed: First iteration where 3-point smoothed loss falls below 0.01; reported as "N/A" if never reached within 5000 iterations - Bit accuracy: Fraction of output bits correctly predicted (threshold at 0.5) at the final checkpoint - Average final accuracy: Mean accuracy over the last 10% of training checkpoints (stability measure)

Analysis

ANALYSIS OF RESULTS 1. BPTT AS GOLD STANDARD BPTT with Adam (lr=0.001) achieves near-perfect performance, providing a strong upper bound for comparison. Its learning trajectory is strikingly rapid and monotonic: Iteration 0: loss=0.7273, accuracy=0.478 (random, untrained) Iteration 50: loss=0.2272, accuracy=0.624 (rapid initial improvement) Iteration 200: loss=0.1469, accuracy=0.771 Iteration 350: loss=0.0645, accuracy=0.931 Iteration 500: loss=0.0111, accuracy=1.000 (effectively solved) Iteration 1000: loss=0.0035, accuracy=1.000 Iteration 5000: loss=0.0004, accuracy=1.000 By iteration 500, BPTT has essentially solved the task with 100% accuracy. The smooth, consistent decline reflects exact gradient information guiding weight updates at every step. This confirms the copy task is solvable by a 128-unit vanilla RNN and that the task difficulty is appropriate for distinguishing method performance. Notably, BPTT is highly sensitive to learning rate. At lr=0.005, BPTT achieves only 60.4% accuracy (MSE=0.231), and at lr=0.01 it reaches only 45.3% (MSE=0.290) -- worse than some biologically plausible methods at their best learning rates. This underscores that BPTT's superiority depends on correct hyperparameter tuning. 2. OPCA LEARNING DYNAMICS: FAST START, PREMATURE PLATEAU OPCA (lr=0.005) shows a qualitatively different learning trajectory from BPTT. It learns quickly in early training but then plateaus and fails to continue improving: Iteration 0: loss=0.7273, accuracy=0.478 Iteration 500: loss=0.1838, accuracy=0.715 (fast early drop) Iteration 1000: loss=0.2405, accuracy=0.574 (loss INCREASES after iter 500) Iteration 2000: loss=0.2402, accuracy=0.595 Iteration 3500: loss=0.2166, accuracy=0.650 (partial recovery) Iteration 4950: loss=0.2339, accuracy=0.624 (final) Two observations are striking. First, OPCA's loss at iteration 500 (0.184) is actually lower than its final loss at iteration 4950 (0.234), meaning performance degrades after early training. The minimum loss achieved (0.122) occurs around iteration 600, but is not sustained. Second, accuracy oscillates substantially -- dropping from 71.5% at iteration 500 to 57.4% at iteration 1000 before partially recovering. This pattern is consistent with a credit signal that provides useful directional information early in training when weights are small and well-conditioned, but becomes increasingly unreliable as weights grow and W itself is used for credit propagation. As W changes during training, the credit pathway also changes, creating a moving target that may interfere with stable learning. However, we caution this is a post-hoc interpretation and would require further controlled experiments to confirm. 3. THE OPCA VS. FA PARADOX: RANDOM BEATS STRUCTURED The most counterintuitive finding is that Feedback Alignment (FA), which uses a fixed random matrix B for credit propagation, consistently outperforms OPCA, which uses the actual forward weight matrix W. Examining the trajectories in detail: Iteration FA loss OPCA loss FA acc OPCA acc --------- --------- --------- ------ -------- 500 0.2181 0.1838 0.664 0.715 1000 0.1864 0.2405 0.736 0.574 2000 0.1792 0.2402 0.752 0.595 4000 0.1540 0.2147 0.785 0.650 4950 0.1453 0.2339 0.797 0.624 OPCA has an early advantage: at iteration 500, its loss (0.184) is lower than FA's (0.218) and its accuracy (71.5%) exceeds FA's (66.4%). However, FA overtakes OPCA between iterations 500 and 1000 and continues improving steadily thereafter, while OPCA stagnates. By iteration 4950, FA achieves 79.7% accuracy versus OPCA's 62.4%. Several hypotheses may explain this finding. First, as W is updated during training, using W for credit propagation creates a coupling between forward dynamics and credit signals -- changes to W simultaneously alter how the network computes and how credit is assigned, potentially creating instability. FA's fixed B avoids this coupling. Second, the spectral properties of a random matrix may provide more stable credit propagation over 26 timesteps than an evolving W. Third, if W develops large singular values during training, credit signals propagated through W may explode or concentrate on dominant modes, reducing gradient diversity. None of these explanations can be confirmed from the current experiments alone. 4. OPCA-ALPHA: THE VALUE OF LEARNED DAMPING The OPCA-alpha variant, which treats the damping factor alpha as a learnable parameter, achieves the best performance among biologically plausible methods. Its trajectory: Iteration 0: loss=0.7273, accuracy=0.478, alpha=0.900 (initialized) Iteration 500: loss=0.1476, accuracy=0.798, alpha~0.3 (rapidly decreasing) Iteration 1000: loss=0.1527, accuracy=0.788 Iteration 2000: loss=0.1555, accuracy=0.782 Iteration 3500: loss=0.1428, accuracy=0.727 Iteration 4950: loss=0.1416, accuracy=0.806, alpha=0.047 (converged) The learned alpha dropped dramatically from 0.900 to 0.047 -- a reduction of 95%. This means that in the final trained model, credit at timestep t receives only 4.7% of the credit from timestep t+1, making the credit propagation nearly local in time. This is in stark contrast to alpha=0.9 for standard OPCA, where credit from a timestep 26 steps away is attenuated by only 0.9^26 = 0.072 -- still non-trivial. The fact that learning drives alpha to near-zero suggests the task benefits from local rather than long-range credit assignment. A plausible explanation: because the input sequence is presented redundantly across 8 timesteps (the same 8 bits repeated), the output layer and nearby hidden units already receive sufficient learning signal from short-range credit. Long-range propagation through the delay period may add noise rather than useful signal when using the non-transposed W. The improved stability of OPCA-alpha (average final accuracy 75.2%) compared to standard OPCA (60.9%) further supports this interpretation, though the average is pulled down by early-training variance before alpha converges. 5. RFLO: THE COST OF UNSTABLE FEEDBACK RFLO, which re-samples a new random feedback matrix at every iteration, performs worst among all methods. Its trajectory reveals instability: Iteration 0: loss=0.7273, accuracy=0.478 Iteration 500: loss=0.2305, accuracy=0.633 (some learning) Iteration 1000: loss=0.3336, accuracy=0.466 (loss INCREASES substantially) Iteration 2000: loss=0.2628, accuracy=0.489 (partial recovery) Iteration 4950: loss=0.2679, accuracy=0.516 (near random performance) The increase in loss from 0.231 at iteration 500 to 0.334 at iteration 1000 is particularly striking -- this is not noise but a consistent pattern across the training run. Each iteration's credit signal points in a different random direction, creating a random walk in weight space that erases progress made in prior iterations. By the end of training, accuracy at 51.6% is only marginally above the 50% random baseline, confirming that consistent gradient direction is essential for RNN learning on this task. This result also serves as an important control: FA's success (79.7%) compared to RFLO's failure (51.6%) demonstrates that a fixed random matrix is far superior to changing random matrices, because the fixed B provides a consistent (if approximate) gradient direction that the network can adapt to over time. This is consistent with the theoretical analysis underlying Feedback Alignment. 6. LEARNING RATE SENSITIVITY AND ITS IMPLICATIONS A noteworthy pattern is that BPTT is far more sensitive to learning rate than the biologically plausible methods: Method lr=0.001 acc lr=0.005 acc lr=0.010 acc Range ------ ------------ ------------ ------------ ----- BPTT 1.000 0.604 0.453 0.547 OPCA 0.596 0.624 0.617 0.028 FA 0.797 0.680 0.619 0.178 OPCA-alpha 0.806 0.465 0.507 0.341 RFLO 0.516 0.500 0.500 0.016 BPTT's performance swings from perfect (100%) to near-chance (45.3%) across the three learning rates tested. OPCA, by contrast, achieves nearly identical performance (59.6% to 62.4%) regardless of learning rate -- suggesting it converges to a similar, sub-optimal solution from different step sizes. This robustness may actually reflect a limitation: OPCA's credit signal may consistently guide weights toward the same local attractor regardless of how large the steps are. RFLO's near-zero sensitivity (51.6% to 50.0%) reflects the fact that with random gradient directions at every step, learning rate only controls the magnitude of random perturbations -- learning effectively fails at all tested rates. 7. OVERALL ASSESSMENT Taken together, the results present a nuanced picture. OPCA does learn -- all methods show meaningful loss reduction from the untrained baseline (0.727) -- but it does not match BPTT's performance on this task. The structured forward weights W do not appear to provide an advantage over random feedback for credit propagation; if anything, they may introduce instability through the coupling between computation and credit. The OPCA-alpha result is the most encouraging: by allowing the algorithm to adapt the temporal extent of credit propagation, performance improves substantially. This suggests that the core mechanism of OPCA (phase-gated credit propagation through forward weights) may have merit, but requires careful tuning of how far credit propagates through time. We emphasize that these conclusions are limited to one task, one architecture, and one set of hyperparameters. The copy task is specifically designed to test long-range temporal memory, which may be a challenging setting for a non-transposed credit propagation rule. Performance on tasks with shorter temporal dependencies, different sequence statistics, or different network architectures could differ substantially. Further experiments are needed before general conclusions about OPCA can be drawn.

Conclusion

This work implements and evaluates the Oscillatory Phase Credit Assignment (OPCA) learning rule on the copy task, a standard RNN benchmark requiring temporal memory. We draw the following conclusions, stated conservatively given the limited scope of the experiments. 1. OPCA LEARNS, BUT FALLS SHORT OF BPTT ON THIS TASK OPCA successfully reduces loss from the random initialization baseline and achieves 62.4% bit accuracy after 5000 training iterations. However, it falls substantially short of BPTT, which achieves 100% accuracy and converges in 650 iterations. The performance gap -- approximately 38 percentage points in accuracy -- is large and consistent across learning rates, suggesting it is not primarily an artifact of suboptimal hyperparameters. Whether this gap reflects a fundamental limitation of OPCA or task-specific factors cannot be determined from these experiments alone. 2. USING FORWARD WEIGHTS W FOR CREDIT DOES NOT OUTPERFORM RANDOM FEEDBACK A central hypothesis of OPCA is that using the forward weight matrix W (rather than a random matrix) for credit propagation should provide better-aligned gradient information. Our experiments do not support this hypothesis on the copy task. Feedback Alignment, which uses a fixed random matrix B, achieves 79.7% accuracy compared to OPCA's 62.4%. This is a notable reversal of the expected ordering. We offer possible explanations -- including coupling instability between W's role in computation and credit propagation -- but cannot confirm any mechanistic account from the current data. This finding warrants further investigation, particularly on tasks with different temporal structure. 3. LEARNED DAMPING (OPCA-ALPHA) IS THE MOST EFFECTIVE BIOLOGICALLY PLAUSIBLE METHOD Allowing the damping factor alpha to be learned rather than fixed improves performance substantially. OPCA-alpha achieves 80.6% accuracy, the best among biologically plausible methods, and its learned alpha converged from 0.9 to 0.047 -- indicating the algorithm discovered that nearly local (short-range) credit propagation is most effective for this task. This suggests that the temporal extent of credit propagation is an important hyperparameter for OPCA-like rules, and that adaptive or task-specific tuning of this parameter may be important. However, the improvement from learned alpha should be interpreted cautiously: OPCA-alpha involves additional complexity (a meta-learned parameter) that reduces direct comparability with fixed-alpha baselines. 4. CONSISTENT FEEDBACK DIRECTION IS CRITICAL The RFLO condition, which re-samples a new random feedback matrix at every iteration, fails to learn effectively (51.6% accuracy, near random chance). This confirms that the consistency of the credit signal across iterations -- not just its approximate directionality -- is essential for learning. FA's fixed random matrix succeeds where RFLO's changing random matrix fails, a result consistent with the theoretical underpinnings of Feedback Alignment. 5. ADAM OPTIMIZATION IS IMPORTANT FOR BIOLOGICALLY PLAUSIBLE METHODS Preliminary experiments showed that plain SGD produces very slow or negligible learning for OPCA and related methods. Using Adam momentum substantially improves performance for all biologically plausible methods. This is worth noting because Adam is not itself biologically plausible. Any claimed biological plausibility of OPCA as tested here is therefore partial -- the learning rule computes credit signals locally, but relies on an adaptive optimizer that is not obviously implementable in neural circuits. Future work should investigate whether simpler momentum mechanisms suffice. 6. LIMITATIONS AND CAVEATS These conclusions are subject to important limitations: - Single task: All comparisons are based on one benchmark (copy task with 8 bits, 10-step delay). Performance may differ substantially on tasks with shorter temporal dependencies, richer input statistics, or larger networks. - Single architecture: Only a vanilla RNN with 128 hidden units was tested. Results may not generalize to LSTMs, GRUs, or larger networks. - Limited training: 5000 iterations may be insufficient for some biologically plausible methods to reach their asymptotic performance. OPCA-alpha's slow but steady improvement suggests longer training might yield better results. - Hyperparameter coverage: Only three learning rates were searched. Other hyperparameters (alpha values, network size, batch size) were not systematically varied. - Implementation choices: The specific formulation of OPCA involves several design decisions (how output error is projected to hidden space, whether to modulate credit by tanh derivative before or after propagation) that may significantly affect performance but were not ablated. 7. OVERALL ASSESSMENT OPCA provides an interesting mechanistic proposal for how biological neural circuits might solve the credit assignment problem using oscillatory phase dynamics. The core idea -- that the same synaptic weights can alternately serve computation and credit propagation depending on the oscillatory phase -- is biologically motivated and theoretically coherent. However, based on the single benchmark tested here, OPCA as implemented does not demonstrate a clear advantage over Feedback Alignment, and falls well short of BPTT. The OPCA-alpha variant shows promise, but the reasons for its improvement (reduced alpha, i.e., more local credit) raise questions about how much the temporal credit propagation mechanism is contributing to learning. We recommend treating these results as preliminary. Definitive evaluation of OPCA would require testing on a broader range of tasks, longer training runs, ablation studies on design choices, and comparison against additional baselines. The current experiments are sufficient to confirm that OPCA learns and to characterize its qualitative behavior on the copy task, but not sufficient to draw broad conclusions about its practical utility or biological significance.
